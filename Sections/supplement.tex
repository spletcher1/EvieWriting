\begin{figure}[p]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.9\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Figs/DDrop_Supp.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{DDrop supplement.} 
Height reached by 9 sec after drop (top plots of each panel) and total up distance climbed in 30 sec after drop (bottom plots). 
Interaction (light $\times$ genotype) p-values were derived from 2-way ANOVAs. 
Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied within each sex and driver and across time points and climbing metrics. 
Points represent average of 3 trials per individual. n = 20-24.
\textbf{(A)} ER4d activation has no effect on climbing ability of females or males. 
\textbf{(B)} Broader EB inhibition has no effect on climbing ability of females or males. 
}
\label{fig:ddropsupp}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[p]
\ContinuedFloat
\caption{\textbf{Self-administration of optogenetic activation in FLIC drives nuanced appetitive and aversive behaviors.}
\textbf{(A, C-E)} P-values were derived from the genotype $\times$ linkage interaction term of a linear mixed model (Outcome ~ Genotype * Linkage + (1|Group), lmer) with yoked pairs modeled as a random intercept.
\textbf{(A)} Naïve ER2>Chr flies spent less time at the S10 well per visit (left), but approached it as often as controls (right). 
n = 13-18. 
\textbf{(B)} (Left) A "trigger" fly (top chamber, ER2>WCS) controlled optogenetic light for itself, a yoked genetic control (middle chamber), and a yoked ER2>Chr fly (bottom chamber) by touching a 10\% sucrose (S10) well. 
(Right) Naïve ER2>Chr flies did not interact with the well more often than yoked genetic controls, indicating no global, non-associative effects of ER2 activation on feeding. 
P-values were derived from planned contrasts on a linear mixed model (Interactions ~ Condition + (1|Group), lmer) with the yoked pairing structure modeled as a random intercept.
n = 16-18. 
\textbf{(C)} Naïve ER4d>GtACR1 interactions were indistinguishable from controls, as all flies were startled by onset of green light. 
n = 13-18. 
\textbf{(D)} Naïve ER4d>Chr trigger flies, like ER2 flies, spent less time at the well per visit (left) but returned as frequently as controls (right). 
n = 13-17. 
\textbf{(E)} ER2/ER4>Chr trigger flies spent the same amount of time per visit at wells (left) but visited more frequently than controls (right). 
n = 10-18.
}
\label{fig:flicsupp}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[p]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.9\textheight,keepaspectratio]{Figs/Arena_Supp.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Stimulation frequency modulates EB-driven valence.} 
Light-naïve flies were placed in lanes with a dark side and an illuminated side (red light at 200 Hz or green light at 40 Hz) and subjected to a two-phase protocol: 1) a 10-min dark acclimation period; 2) a 60-min treatment of 5 sec on/5 sec off. Reported p-values for Preference Index correspond to genotype $\times$ phase interaction term. 
\textbf{(A)} ER2/ER4>Chr females and males preferred the lit side. n = 34-36. 
\textbf{(B)} ER2>Chr females preferred the unlit side. Males were indistinguishable from controls. n = 35-36.
\textbf{(C)} ER4d>GtACR1 females (n = 26-27) and males (n = 17-18) were indistinguishable from controls. 
\textbf{(D)} ER4d>Chr females and males were indistinguishable from controls. n = 34-36.
}
\label{fig:arenasupp}
\end{figure}
